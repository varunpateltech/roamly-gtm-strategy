> Module 6 · Launch — ★ Deliverable 6

## 1. Launch plan

| Phase | Goal | Key activities | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-launch (Beta)** | Validate early, settle the free-core pricing assumption before launch | Invite power users to test group booking. Run Van Westendorp on these organizers now, before marketing copy locks. Brief support first (self-serve arrives first). Brief CS on what's promised, for the rare large group that needs a human touch. Seed early advocates in travel communities (CLG layer). | Product + Support + CS |
| **Launch** | Drive awareness through the invite loop | Ship in-app notification, email, and social asset. Owned channels only. If invite-to-signup is under 30% by week 4, test paid social. | Product + Marketing |
| **Post-launch** | Sustain momentum | Track invite-to-signup and completion rate weekly. Expand to paid only if the week-4 trigger fires. Collect testimonials. Flag repeat/large-group organizers for the CS handoff. | Product + Growth |

## 2. Launch metrics & guardrails

- **Success looks like:** ~30% invite-to-signup within 7 days, ~60% group-booking completion, both at 90 days.
- **Tripwire (when to pull back):** Completion well under 30% after 4 weeks, pause rollout and revisit the product, not just marketing.

## 3. The full GTM story in one slide

> Roamly Groups wins by finishing what planning-only tools can't, turning a shared plan into a booked, paid activity. It targets friend groups first through a hybrid motion, self-serve invites driving most growth, community sharing amplifying it. Positioning and messaging lead with removing the coordination burden. Pricing keeps the core free to protect that invite behavior, validated with real willingness-to-pay research before launch, not assumed. The launch enables support and CS first, and starts with owned channels, holding paid in reserve until the data says it's needed.

## 4. Individual insights

_This is the reflection you share. Be honest and specific._

- **Friction points:** Figuring out how to price the core booking. I kept defaulting to "charge something for the coordination itself" because that felt like the obvious way to monetize a new feature. What worked through it was going back to the pricing goal I'd already set, entering a new market, not maximizing revenue, and asking what a charge on the core booking would actually cost me: it would tax the exact invite behavior the whole acquisition loop depends on. Once I separated "what Roamly charges for" from "what the group pays each other," the packaging became simple: free core, paid add-ons on top. I also realized I'd been unclear on who even sees a price, the organizer picks a tier, the invited member never does, and naming that fixed the confusion.
- **Biggest "aha" across the certification:** My aha moment came in Module 2. Researching Wanderlog showed it already owns trip planning, it just can't book or take payment. That reframed everything: Roamly Groups doesn't need to out-plan anyone, it needs to be the one that finishes the job. That single distinction shaped my positioning (narrowing "shared itinerary planning" to something honest about scope), my messaging (leading with "book it once," not "plan together"), and my pricing (keeping the core booking free to protect exactly what Wanderlog can't do).
- **What changed in how you think about GTM:** The biggest shift for me was seeing how one fuzzy call early on doesn't stay contained, it resurfaces two modules later as someone else's problem. The competitive gap I found in Module 2 became a positioning scope issue in Module 3, then a messaging gap in Module 4. I used to treat GTM strategy, positioning, messaging, and pricing as separate exercises. Now I see them as one decision, repeated in different formats, and a weak spot in any one of them shows up in all the others eventually.
- **What you'd do differently with more time / data:** I'd commission the Van Westendorp research before finalizing the packaging, not after. I locked in "free core, paid add-ons" on a defensible assumption, but it's still an assumption. I'd also want real group-booking behavior once the feature is live, not just the proxy signals (support tickets, clustered bookings) I had to rely on for a feature that doesn't exist yet.
- **The one idea you'll apply at work on Monday:** Before committing to any pricing or packaging call, name explicitly which audience actually sees and chooses each part of it. I almost shipped a packaging plan without realizing only one of my two audiences ever interacts with a tier, the other just sees the outcome. That single question, who's actually making this decision, would have saved me a wrong turn earlier if I'd asked it first.
